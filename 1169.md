## Introduction to the Course in English
Prompt engineering is crafting precise, context-rich inputs to large language models such as OpenAI ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot, and Google Gemini to generate useful, accurate, and relevant outputs. It is important because well-designed prompts unlock the full potential of AI, enabling businesses to automate analyses, drive insights, and enhance decision-making.

By the end of this course, students will be able to explain the fundamental principles and components of prompt engineering and how large language models interpret and generate responses. They will develop the skills to design clear, specific, and context-rich prompts tailored to key business-management scenarios such as marketing, finance, operations, and human resources. Through hands-on practice and iterative refinement, students will learn to evaluate the quality of AI outputs, identify biases or errors, and implement strategies for continuous prompt optimization. They will integrate prompt-driven workflows into real-world business processes, confidently automating routine analyses and report generation. Finally, students will demonstrate responsible and ethical use of AI by recognizing potential risks, ensuring data privacy, and establishing guardrails to mitigate bias in their applications.

### Student Guide: Saving and Using Web Data (CSV & TSV Files)
Many sessions in this course utilize real public business data stored in CSV (Comma-Separated Values) or TSV (Tab-Separated Values) formats. These are lightweight, plain-text spreadsheet formats used throughout the business world. Follow this step-by-step guide to download and use these files easily:

1. **How to Download and Save from a Web URL:**
   - **Method A (Direct Link Download):** On the dataset link provided in the session, right-click the link and select **"Save link as..."** (Chrome/Edge) or **"Save target as..."**. Select a destination folder on your computer (e.g., `Documents/AI_Course/`), make sure the file name ends in `.csv` or `.tsv`, and click **Save**.
   - **Method B (When Browser Displays Raw Text):** If clicking a link opens a page displaying raw text with commas or tabs instead of downloading, press **Ctrl + S** (Windows) or **Cmd + S** (Mac). In the save dialog, change "Save as type" to **All Files (*.*)**, ensure the file extension is `.csv` or `.tsv` (do not let it save as `.txt` or `.html`), and click **Save**.
2. **How to Open and Inspect the Data:**
   - **Spreadsheet Software (Excel / Google Sheets):** Double-click the downloaded file to view it in Microsoft Excel, or open Google Sheets and go to `File > Import > Upload` to inspect the columns cleanly.
   - **Plain Text Editor (Notepad / TextEdit):** Right-click the file and select **Open with > Notepad** to view the raw data rows without automatic spreadsheet formatting.
3. **How to Ingest Sample Data into ChatGPT:**
   - Open your spreadsheet or text file.
   - **Always Include the Header Row:** Highlight the top header row containing the column names (e.g., `order_id`, `quantity`, `item_name`, `item_price`) plus the specific number of sample rows requested in the exercise (e.g., rows 1 to 20).
   - **Use Text Delimiters:** In ChatGPT, enclose the pasted rows inside clear symbols (such as `###` or `"""`) so the model understands where your instructions end and the raw data begins.

#### Session 1.1: Getting Started with ChatGPT in Business

![A business support manager checks an AI-written reply against a company policy document and asks, "Should we trust a fluent AI answer without checking the facts?" The illustration uses Korean layered-hanji paper-cut forms and jogakbo-inspired patchwork.](images/session_1_1_fundamentals_hanji.png)

* **Lecture Presentation:** [Download Slides (PDF)](slides/session_1_1_lecture.pdf)

- **Duration:** 3 Hours (180 minutes)
  - *Hour 1 (50 min):* Comprehensive Concept Lecture & Live Demonstration
  - *Hour 2 (50 min):* Guided Hands-On Lab (Basic Exercise: Customer Review Comparison)
  - *Hour 3 (50 min):* Applied Business Challenge & Peer Debrief (Advanced Exercise: Multi-Issue Escalation)
- **Goal:** Learn what ChatGPT can and cannot do, understand how it generates text, and build safety habits for daily office tasks.
- **Lecture Content (Hour 1 - 50 Minutes):**
  - **Part 1: How LLMs Actually Work (15 min):**  
    *The Predictive Autocomplete Analogy:* Most business professionals mistakenly believe ChatGPT searches an encyclopedia database or "thinks" like a human. In reality, Large Language Models predict the next most likely word token based on statistical patterns learned from vast training datasets. When an LLM generates text, it calculates probability distributions across billions of parameters. Because it generates text probabilistically rather than retrieving hard records, it produces fluent language, but has no inherent sense of truth.
  - **Part 2: ChatGPT vs. Google Search - The Strategic Distinction (15 min):**  
    *Decision Framework:* Teach students when to search vs. when to prompt.
    - *Use Traditional Search (Google):* For verifiable historical facts, live stock quotes, statutory regulations, telephone numbers, and cited scientific research.
    - *Use Generative AI (ChatGPT):* For drafting executive correspondence, translating tone (e.g., technical jargon to layperson English), summarizing unformatted text, and brainstorming strategic angles.
    - *The Modern Business Hybrid Workflow:* (1) Gather verified facts from primary company sources -> (2) Feed those verified facts into ChatGPT to draft and format the communication.
    
    ![Session 1.1 Conceptual Framework: Search Engine vs Generative AI Decision Matrix and Hybrid Business Workflow](images/session_1_1_key_concept.png)
  - **Part 3: The Hallucination Problem & Enterprise Risk (10 min):**  
    *What is a Hallucination?* Plausibly sounding, highly confident statements generated by AI that are completely false.  
    *Case Study - Air Canada Lawsuit (2024):* An Air Canada customer service chatbot invented a non-existent bereavement refund policy. A customer relied on the chatbot's advice and sued when the refund was refused. The Canadian tribunal ruled Air Canada legally liable for its chatbot's hallucination.  
    *Managerial Golden Rule:* AI is a brilliant junior copywriter, but a terrible factual auditor. Never approve an AI-generated document without personally verifying dates, names, and calculations.
  - **Part 4: Instructor Live Demonstration Script & Discussion (10 min):**  
    *Step 1 (Naive Request):* Type `"Write an apology email to an angry customer whose delivery was late."` Note the empty platitudes (*"We value your business"*) and lack of actionable next steps.  
    *Step 2 (Engineered Prompt):* Type a structured prompt specifying Role (*"Customer Support Lead"*), Context (*"4-day courier delay on subscription coffee"*), Task (*"Draft 3-paragraph email"*), Constraints (*"Acknowledge delay in sentence 1; offer $10 credit code [COFFEE10]; explain backup carrier policy in 2 bullets; under 150 words"*).  
    *Discussion Question:* *"Why does setting a specific persona (like 'Senior Operations Lead') improve AI output quality?"* (Answer: It narrows the model's vocabulary and probability distribution toward professional domain terminology).
- **Public Data Source:** [PyCaret Open Amazon Customer Reviews Dataset (CSV)](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pycaret/pycaret/master/datasets/amazon.csv) - Real open customer reviews and ratings.
- **Reference:** [Microsoft & LinkedIn: Work Trend Index Annual Report (2024)](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/ai-at-work-is-here-now-comes-the-hard-part) - Global enterprise study on AI workplace adoption, productivity metrics, and corporate risk.
- **Prompt Comparison:**
  - **Weak Prompt Example (Vague & Unstructured):**
    ```text
    Write an apology email to an angry customer whose delivery was late.
    ```
    *Why it fails:* Lacks role, operational context, order details, and constraints. ChatGPT defaults to hollow platitudes ("We value your business") with no actionable resolution.
  - **Strong Prompt Example (Structured & Constrained):**
    ```text
    You are the Customer Support Lead for an online artisanal coffee subscription brand.
    A long-time subscriber experienced a 4-day courier delay due to regional weather.

    Write a 3-paragraph apology email following these rules:
    1. Acknowledge and apologize for the shipping delay in the very first sentence.
    2. Offer a $10 store credit code [COFFEE10] toward their next monthly renewal.
    3. Explain our backup carrier routing policy in 2 concise bullet points.
    4. Maintain an empathetic, professional tone and keep the total length under 150 words.
    ```
    *Why it succeeds:* Establishes an authoritative persona, realistic business context, clear monetary resolution, and strict length/structure boundaries.
- **How to Practice:**
  1. **Step 1:** Open the public dataset link and copy 2 real customer reviews (one positive review and one complaint).
  2. **Step 2:** In ChatGPT, paste the reviews and enter a basic prompt: *"Write a customer service reply to these reviews."* Notice how generic the reply sounds.
  3. **Step 3:** In a new chat window, enter an engineered prompt: *"You are the Customer Support Lead for an online retail brand. Write a polite, empathetic, 3-paragraph reply apologizing for the issue, offering a replacement order, and explaining our quality check process. Tone: Warm and professional."*
  4. **Step 4:** Compare Output A and Output B side by side. Record your evaluation in a scorecard table for your report.
- **What to Expect:** See how giving clear background information produces much more relevant, professional, and practical answers.
- **Exercise 1 (Basic - Customer Review Response Scorecard):**
  - Scenario: Using the PyCaret Amazon Customer Reviews dataset from this session, select one 5-star positive review and one 1-star negative complaint.
  - Task: Test a naive prompt versus the engineered Customer Support Lead prompt provided in the lesson.
  - Deliverable: A Prompt Comparison Scorecard table evaluating both outputs across usefulness, accuracy, and executive tone (1-to-5 scale), accompanied by a 1-sentence diagnostic note.
- **Exercise 2 (Advanced - Multi-Issue Escalation & Internal Alert):**
  - Scenario: From the same Amazon Customer Reviews dataset, identify a complex negative review that cites multiple simultaneous operational failures (e.g., damaged product packaging, a delayed shipment, and an unresponsive delivery courier).
  - Task: Engineer an advanced prompt instructing ChatGPT to act as a Senior Customer Operations Lead. Apply strict negative constraints (no excuses, no blaming shipping couriers), propose a tiered resolution (immediate product replacement plus a promotional discount code), and generate a simultaneous 3-bullet internal operational alert for the warehouse fulfillment team.
  - Deliverable: A two-part deliverable: (1) A polished, empathetic customer reply under 150 words, and (2) A 3-bullet internal operational escalation note.

- **Report:** Submit one report for this session using the [report template](Report_template.docx). File name: `FirstName_StudentID_W1_Keyword.docx`.
  - **Introduction:** Explain why a generic AI reply to a customer review can hurt the business, and what you want a better prompt to do.

#### Session 1.2: Structuring Your Prompts for Success

![An employee turns a company press release into a briefing with a four-part prompt labeled Role, Context, Task, and Format, asking, "How can one prompt produce a useful business briefing?" The illustration uses Korean layered-hanji paper-cut forms and jogakbo-inspired patchwork.](images/session_1_2_prompt_architecture_hanji.png)

* **Lecture Presentation:** [Download Slides (PDF)](slides/session_1_2_lecture.pdf)

- **Duration:** 3 Hours (180 minutes)
  - *Hour 1 (50 min):* Comprehensive Concept Lecture & Live Demonstration
  - *Hour 2 (50 min):* Guided Hands-On Lab (Basic Exercise: 4-Box Architecture Form)
  - *Hour 3 (50 min):* Applied Business Challenge & Peer Debrief (Advanced Exercise: Competitive Sales Battlecard)
- **Goal:** Master the 4-part prompt formula to consistently generate executive-ready business deliverables.
- **Lecture Content (Hour 1 - 50 Minutes):**
  - **Part 1: The Cost of Unstructured Prompts in Business (15 min):**  
    When employees give ambiguous prompts, ChatGPT generates an "average" response that requires multiple cycles of manual editing. Every cycle wastes valuable business hours. Prompt architecture provides the model with the exact structural boundaries it needs to produce workplace-ready output on the very first try.
  - **Part 2: The 4-Box Prompt Architecture Framework (15 min):**  
    Teach the 4 modular building blocks:
    1. **Role / Persona:** Establishes the authoritative viewpoint, tone, and professional vocabulary (e.g., *"Senior Product Marketing Director"*).
    2. **Business Context:** The operational backdrop, target customer, problem being solved, and market conditions.
    3. **Task Directive:** An unambiguous command verb stating the exact deliverable (e.g., *"Draft a launch announcement memo"*).
    4. **Output Format Template:** The exact schema, headings, bullet counts, and sections required.
  - **Part 3: Delimiters and Modular Prompt Design (10 min):**  
    Why delimiters matter: Using symbols like `###`, `---`, or `"""` clearly separates instructions from raw data. This prevents the model from confusing text to analyze with commands to execute, establishing clean prompt hygiene.

    ![Session 1.2 Conceptual Framework: The 4-Box Prompt Architecture and Text Delimiters](images/session_1_2_key_concept.png)

  - **Part 4: Instructor Live Demonstration Script & Discussion (10 min):**  
    *Live Demo:* Extract 4 facts from a corporate press release on PR Newswire. Type a 4-box modular prompt live on screen into ChatGPT to draft an internal company briefing. Show how defining the Output Format Template ensures the AI never outputs conversational pleasantries like *"Sure, here is your email!"*.  
    *Discussion Question:* *"If you omit the Output Format Template, what default formatting mistakes does ChatGPT typically make?"* (Answer: It produces rambling introductory paragraphs, inconsistent bullet points, and excessive sign-offs).
- **Public Data Source:** [PR Newswire Public Business Press Releases](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/) - Free, real-world corporate product launch announcements and press releases.
- **Reference:** [OpenAI Official Prompt Engineering Guide](https://platform.openai.com/docs/guides/prompt-engineering) - Industry baseline standard for modular prompt architecture (delimiters, personas, context, and output schemas).
- **Prompt Comparison:**
  - **Weak Prompt Example (Vague & Unstructured):**
    ```text
    Summarize this corporate press release for our internal staff memo.
    ```
    *Why it fails:* ChatGPT will guess the audience, produce rambling paragraphs, include conversational filler ("Sure, here is your summary!"), and omit key business metrics.
  - **Strong Prompt Example (4-Box Architecture & Delimiters):**
    ```text
    Role: Senior Product Marketing Director.
    Business Context: We are preparing an internal product briefing for our regional sales reps regarding our competitor's new enterprise cloud software announcement.
    Task Directive: Extract key competitive facts from the press release delimited below and organize them into an executive briefing memo.

    Input Press Release:
    ###
    [Paste PR Newswire text here]
    ###

    Output Format Template:
    - Executive Headline: [1 sentence]
    - Core Customer Problem Solved: [2 bullets]
    - Target Market & Pricing: [1-2 bullets]
    - Threat Level & Sales Counter-Arguments: [3 numbered bullets]
    Constraint: Do NOT include conversational pleasantries or introductory greetings.
    ```
    *Why it succeeds:* Uses the 4-box architecture (Role, Context, Task, Format) with delimiters (###) to prevent instruction bleed and guarantee zero filler.
- **How to Practice:**
  1. **Step 1:** Open the PR Newswire page and click on any recent product launch announcement.
  2. **Step 2:** Read the article and extract 4 key facts: company name, product name, main customer problem solved, and target audience.
  3. **Step 3:** Build your prompt by explicitly filling in the 4 boxes: `[Role]`, `[Business Context]`, `[Task Directive]`, and `[Sample Output Template]`.
  4. **Step 4:** Copy your complete 4-part prompt into ChatGPT. Review the output to confirm whether it followed your exact output structure without extra conversational filler.
- **What to Expect:** Receive an answer that matches professional business formats without needing to rewrite it multiple times.
- **Exercise 1 (Basic - 4-Box Architecture Executive Memo):**
  - Scenario: Select one recent corporate product launch announcement from the PR Newswire public feed.
  - Task: Extract 4 core business facts (company name, product name, customer pain point, target market) and construct a prompt using the 4-Box framework (Role, Context, Task, Format) with delimiters to draft an internal company briefing.
  - Deliverable: A 4-box table and the formatted executive memo.
- **Exercise 2 (Advanced - Competitive Sales Battlecard & Objection Handler):**
  - Scenario: Using the exact same PR Newswire product announcement from Exercise 1, assume your company is the direct market competitor to the announcing firm.
  - Task: Write an advanced modular prompt instructing ChatGPT to act as a Director of Competitive Intelligence. Analyze the announcement to construct a 1-page "Sales Battlecard" for your sales reps containing: (1) Competitor Claim vs. Market Reality check, (2) Top 3 customer purchasing objections your reps will hear, and (3) 3 counter-arguments highlighting your product's competitive advantage.
  - Deliverable: A structured, schema-compliant Sales Battlecard ready to distribute to a sales team.

- **Report:** Submit one report for this session using the [report template](Report_template.docx). File name: `FirstName_StudentID_W2_Keyword.docx`.
  - **Introduction:** Describe the product launch you chose and the briefing your company needs.
  - **Method:** Copy your 4-box prompt from Exercise 1 and your Director of Competitive Intelligence prompt from Exercise 2.
  - **Results:** Include a 4-box table showing what you wrote for Role, Business Context, Task Directive, and Sample Output Template. Add the executive memo and the Sales Battlecard.
  - **Conclusion:** State whether the AI followed your output structure, and one change that would make the prompt more reliable.

#### Session 1.3: Writing Clear, Step-by-Step Instructions

![A store manager edits an overlong AI summary into three short numbered points and an Action for Today, asking, "How can we make this summary short, clear, and useful?" The illustration uses Korean layered-hanji paper-cut forms and jogakbo-inspired patchwork.](images/session_1_3_constraints_hanji.png)

* **Lecture Presentation:** [Download Slides (PDF)](slides/session_1_3_lecture.pdf)

- **Duration:** 3 Hours (180 minutes)
  - *Hour 1 (50 min):* Comprehensive Concept Lecture & Live Demonstration
  - *Hour 2 (50 min):* Guided Hands-On Lab (Basic Exercise: 5-Constraint Summary Audit)
  - *Hour 3 (50 min):* Applied Business Challenge & Peer Debrief (Advanced Exercise: Bimodal Stakeholder Tone Calibration)
- **Goal:** Eliminate ambiguous phrasing and enforce strict negative and positive constraints on AI outputs.
- **Lecture Content (Hour 1 - 50 Minutes):**
  - **Part 1: The Power of Constraints in Executive Writing (15 min):**  
    Executives do not read long paragraphs. They scan for metrics, decisions, and deadlines. Generative AI naturally defaults to wordy, passive-voice prose. To make AI useful for leadership communication, managers must apply strict constraints that eliminate unnecessary words.
  - **Part 2: Positive vs. Negative Constraints (15 min):**  
    - *Positive Constraints (What to Include):* Format (e.g., *"Exactly 3 numbered bullet points"*), Length (e.g., *"Under 120 words"*), Target audience (e.g., *"Written for a first-time store manager"*), Required data (e.g., *"Must cite the $50 budget limit"*).
    - *Negative Constraints (What to Forbid):* Explicitly banning corporate fluff, clichés, and buzzwords (*"Do NOT use words like 'synergy', 'game-changer', 'delve', 'testament', or 'seamless'"*).
  - **Part 3: Calibrating Tone for Stakeholders (10 min):**  
    Demonstrate how tone constraints alter vocabulary:
    - *CFO Tone:* Quantitative, concise, risk-focused, zero adjectives.
    - *Frontline Employee Tone:* Empathetic, direct, step-by-step, actionable.

    ![Session 1.3 Conceptual Framework: Positive Guidance vs Negative Constraints](images/session_1_3_key_concept.png)

  - **Part 4: Instructor Live Demonstration Script & Discussion (10 min):**  
    *Live Demo:* Take a dense 400-word business operations policy from the SBA guide. Prompt ChatGPT without rules (producing 300 words of generic prose). Then prompt with 5 strict constraints. Count the words live on screen and show how negative constraints force crisp, high-density business communication.  
    *Discussion Question:* *"Why is it harder for AI to write under 100 words than over 500 words?"* (Answer: Compressing ideas requires prioritization and omission, whereas expanding text aligns with the model's natural statistical verbosity).
- **Public Data Source:** [US Small Business Administration (SBA) Business Guide](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/manage-your-business) - Free public business operating guides and management guidelines.
- **Reference:** [HBS Working Paper 24-013 / BCG Study: Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4573321) - Field experiment on 758 BCG consultants showing why negative constraints and rules prevent superficial corporate AI fluff.
- **Prompt Comparison:**
  - **Weak Prompt Example (Vague & Unstructured):**
    ```text
    Give me some tips on how to manage retail store employees effectively.
    ```
    *Why it fails:* Produces a 500-word generic textbook essay filled with clichés ("foster communication", "lead by example") that no busy store manager has time to read.
  - **Strong Prompt Example (Positive & Negative Constraints):**
    ```text
    You are a Retail Operations Consultant. Summarize the employee management guidelines below for a first-time store manager.

    Follow these 5 strict constraints:
    1. Maximum length: Exactly under 120 words total.
    2. Format: Exactly 3 numbered bullet points, followed by one single "Action for Today".
    3. Tone: Direct, operational, and practical (no academic jargon).
    4. Negative Constraint: Do NOT use corporate buzzwords such as "synergy", "paradigm shift", "game-changer", "delve", or "testament".
    5. Required Content: Every bullet must include a specific operational behavior (e.g., morning shift huddles, checklist sign-offs).

    Source Guidelines:
    """
    [Paste SBA guide excerpt here]
    """
    ```
    *Why it succeeds:* Applies strict negative constraints to ban corporate fluff and enforces a tight word count and format, delivering high-density executive prose.
- **How to Practice:**
  1. **Step 1:** Open the SBA guide link and copy 2 paragraphs on managing business operations or staff.
  2. **Step 2:** In ChatGPT, test an unconstrained prompt: *"Summarize this text for my store."* Observe that the summary is too long and unstructured.
  3. **Step 3:** Write a constrained prompt: *"Summarize the text above under these 5 strict rules: (1) Maximum 120 words total, (2) Exactly 3 numbered bullet points, (3) Written for a first-time store manager, (4) Do NOT use words like 'synergy' or 'game-changer', and (5) Include one clear action step to take today."*
  4. **Step 4:** Run the constrained prompt. Audit the output line by line and mark Pass or Fail for each rule in a rule checklist table for your report.
- **What to Expect:** ChatGPT will follow your rules closely, giving a concise answer without unnecessary extra text.
- **Exercise 1 (Basic - 5-Constraint Summary Audit):**
  - Scenario: Copy two paragraphs on employee management from the US Small Business Administration (SBA) guide.
  - Task: Formulate and run a constrained prompt enforcing 5 strict rules: (1) Maximum 120 words, (2) Exactly 3 numbered bullets, (3) First-time manager tone, (4) Negative constraint banning buzzwords ("synergy", "game-changer"), and (5) One concrete action step.
  - Deliverable: A rule checklist table marking Pass or Fail for each rule against the AI output.
- **Exercise 2 (Advanced - Bimodal Stakeholder Tone Calibration):**
  - Scenario: Using the exact same SBA employee management text from Exercise 1, your company must communicate this policy change to two contrasting stakeholder groups: the Chief Financial Officer and frontline hourly staff.
  - Task: Engineer a single master prompt that ingests the SBA text and produces two distinct, tone-calibrated deliverables:
    - Deliverable A (CFO Executive Brief): Strict 80-word ceiling, zero adjectives, quantitative, focused entirely on compliance cost and liability reduction.
    - Deliverable B (Frontline Employee Guide): Step-by-step operational checklist written at an 8th-grade reading level, encouraging, clear, and focused on daily shift routines.

#### Session 1.4: Step-by-Step Problem Solving

![A supermarket owner compares the two existing self-checkout kiosks with a pilot option for two more while staffed lanes remain open and a customer receives assistance. The owner asks, "Should we test two more kiosks and keep staffed lanes open?" in a Korean layered-hanji paper-cut illustration.](images/session_1_4_problem_hanji.png)

* **Lecture Presentation:** [Download Slides (PDF)](slides/session_1_4_lecture.pdf)

- **Duration:** 3 Hours (180 minutes)
  - *Hour 1 (50 min):* CoT concept lesson, prompt comparison, and live demonstration
  - *Hour 2 (50 min):* Guided assumption audit using a self-checkout case dataset
  - *Hour 3 (50 min):* Simple pilot review and improvement plan

- **Problem this session solves:** How can the supermarket reduce its peak-hour queue time while preserving accessible customer service and controlling cost and risk? The store already has two self-checkout kiosks and staffed lanes. The current decision is whether to add two more kiosks for a 90-day pilot while keeping the staffed lanes open. The current average queue is 7.5 minutes during the busiest period. A one-line AI question can produce a confident recommendation without enough evidence about queue time, cost, shrinkage, or customer access.
- **Goal:** Use Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting as the solution method: define the problem, separate evidence from assumptions, compare alternatives, and test the most promising option before scaling it.
- **Practice dataset:** [Self-checkout pilot dataset (CSV)](data/session_1_4_self_checkout_pilot.csv). This is fictional instructional data created for this exercise. It is not a forecast or a real company record.

- **Lecture Content (Hour 1 - 50 Minutes):**
  - **Part 1: What CoT is and why it is useful (15 min):**

    CoT is a request for a structured decision rationale, not a request to reveal private model reasoning. In this course, students use it to make the business analysis inspectable.

    - **Define the decision:** State the owner, deadline, alternatives, and success metric.

    - **Diagnose the evidence:** Separate facts supplied in the case from assumptions and missing information.

    - **Compare alternatives:** Assess each option using the same criteria, such as queue time, total operating cost, shrinkage risk, staff workload, and service access.

    - **Test before scaling:** Give a conditional recommendation with a pilot target and a stop rule.

    CoT is useful because a business recommendation is only credible when a student can explain what evidence supports it and what new evidence would change it.

  - **Part 2: What happens without a structured rationale (10 min):**

    A one-line question often forces the model to fill gaps with plausible but unverified details.

    - **False certainty:** The response may give an exact profit forecast even though no traffic, cost, or loss data were supplied.

    - **Hidden assumptions:** The response may assume customer acceptance, reliable technology, or adequate staff training without naming those assumptions.

    - **Unequal comparison:** One option may receive detailed benefits while another option receives only a vague description.

    - **No operating plan:** A recommendation may say "implement" without defining a pilot period, owner, target, or stop rule.

  - **Part 3: The four-stage CoT decision frame (15 min):**

    1. **Define:** What decision is being made? Who owns it? What outcome counts as success?

    2. **Diagnose:** Which facts are verified? Which assumptions must be tested? What information is missing?

    3. **Compare:** What are the realistic options? What are the benefits, trade-offs, and risks under the same criteria?

    4. **Test:** What 90-day pilot will provide evidence? What target means continue? What stop rule means pause or redesign?

    ![Session 1.4 Conceptual Framework: Chain-of-Thought Reasoning and Pre-Mortem Risk Audit](images/session_1_4_key_concept.png)

  - **Part 4: Instructor live demonstration (10 min):**

    Show the class the fictional self-checkout dataset. Ask students to predict which fields matter before they run a prompt.

    - **Known facts:** 84 peak-hour transactions per hour, 7.5-minute average queue time, two proposed kiosks, $1,150 monthly lease per kiosk, $250 monthly support cost, 1.2% baseline shrinkage, and 20% of peak shoppers aged 65 or older.

    - **Pilot thresholds:** Average queue time at or below five minutes, kiosk uptime at or above 98%, and shrinkage at or below 1.4% of sales.

    - **Discussion question:** Which of these values is a fact, which is a target, and which value would you still need before approving a full rollout?

- **Prompt comparison: two poor uses of CoT**

  - **Poor example 1: No decision frame**

    ```text
    Should our grocery store add two more self-checkout kiosks while keeping staffed lanes open?
    ```

    - No facts, options, deadline, or success metric are supplied.

    - The model can only offer a generic opinion.

    - A student cannot audit whether the recommendation fits this store.

  - **Poor example 2: False certainty**

    ```text
    We have 12 employees. Tell us exactly how much profit two kiosks will add next year and guarantee that customers will accept them.
    ```

    - It requests an exact forecast without required financial or traffic data.

    - It asks for a guarantee where uncertainty is central.

    - It rewards fabricated numbers instead of a plan to collect evidence.

- **Prompt comparison: two effective uses of CoT**

  - **Good example 1: Diagnose before deciding**

    ```text
    You are helping a supermarket owner evaluate adding two more self-checkout kiosks while keeping staffed lanes open.

    Known facts:
    - 12 employees and two existing kiosks
    - Goal: reduce peak-hour queues
    - Concerns: theft, downtime, and service access

    Create three labeled lists:
    1. Facts provided
    2. Assumptions that need validation
    3. Data needed before deciding

    Do not estimate costs or benefits that are not in the facts.
    End with five questions for the owner.
    ```

    - It separates what is known from what still needs evidence.

    - It prevents unsupported financial claims.

    - It gives the student a field-research checklist.

  - **Good example 2: Compare options and design a pilot**

    ```text
    You are a retail operations analyst.
    Use only the Session 1.4 self-checkout pilot dataset.

    Compare these options:
    - Keep the current checkout model
    - Add two more kiosks for 90 days while keeping staffed lanes open
    - Improve service without adding kiosks

    Evaluate every option using:
    - Customer wait time
    - Total operating cost
    - Shrinkage risk
    - Staff workload
    - Service access for older shoppers

    Recommend one option.
    State the assumptions that drive the recommendation,
    one 90-day pilot target, and one stop rule.
    If a required value is absent, write "Information needed" instead of guessing.
    ```

    - It identifies the exact practice dataset instead of asking students to paste imaginary values.

    - It applies one comparison standard to every option.

    - It requires a target and stop rule before a full rollout.

- **Guided practice (Hour 2 - 50 minutes): Exercise 1 - Assumption audit**

  1. Download the [self-checkout pilot dataset](data/session_1_4_self_checkout_pilot.csv).

  2. Run Good Example 1 with the case facts.

  3. Make a three-column table: `Fact`, `Assumption to Test`, and `Information Needed`.

  4. Place at least three items in each column.

  5. Submit the table plus five questions you would ask the store owner.

  - **Success check:** A fact comes from the dataset. An assumption is plausible but unverified. An information need is a value that would materially change the decision.

- **Applied challenge (Hour 3 - 50 minutes): Exercise 2 - Simple pilot review**

  - **Scenario:** The 90-day pilot has finished. The target queue time was five minutes or less. The actual average queue time was 6.5 minutes. Several customers said they needed help using the kiosks during the busiest hour.

  - **What this means:** The pilot did not meet its queue-time target. This does not automatically mean that kiosks are a bad idea. It means the store should identify one practical change and test it before expanding the program.

  - **Step 1 - Name the problem:** Write one clear sentence: "The peak-hour queue is 6.5 minutes, which is above the five-minute target."

  - **Step 2 - Choose one possible cause:** Select one simple cause, such as unclear kiosk instructions, too few staff available to help, or customers needing more time to learn the system.

  - **Step 3 - Choose one improvement:** Select one action, such as placing one employee near the kiosks during peak hours, adding a short instruction sign, or keeping one staffed lane open.

  - **Step 4 - Choose one metric:** Measure the average peak-hour queue time again for two weeks. The target remains five minutes or less.

#### Session 2.1: Read Business Data Before Deciding

<a id="session-21-making-sense-of-business-data"></a>

![Illustration: The young owner of a small bubble tea shop stands behind the counter with a laptop. * **Lecture Presentation:** [Download Slides (PDF)](slides/session_2_1_lecture.pdf)

- **Duration:** 3 hours: 50-minute lesson, 10-minute break, 50-minute guided practice (Exercise 1), 10-minute break, 50-minute applied practice (Exercise 2), and 10-minute wrap-up.
- **Problem:** Mina runs a small bubble tea shop near a university. Her root problem is the lunch line: many students arrive at the same time, staff must prepare five different drinks, and customers wait too long. Mina's idea is to remove one drink from the menu so each order is faster, without losing much profit. She asks ChatGPT, "Which drink should I remove?" ChatGPT answers with confidence: "Remove Mango Fruit Tea, because it sells the fewest cups." But Mango Fruit Tea earns the most money per cup. The AI looked at the wrong number because the question did not tell it what matters.
- **Goal:** Learn how to give business data to an AI tool so that it reads the data correctly, calculates the right measure, and separates facts from guesses before it recommends a decision.
- **Practice data:** [Download the bubble tea sales CSV](data/session_2_1_bubble_tea_sales.csv). This is fictional data created for this class. It shows one week of sales for the five drinks on the menu.

  | drink | cups_sold | price_per_cup_USD | ingredient_cost_per_cup_USD |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Brown Sugar Milk Tea | 420 | 5.00 | 2.40 |
  | Classic Milk Tea | 380 | 4.50 | 1.60 |
  | Matcha Latte | 160 | 5.50 | 2.10 |
  | Taro Smoothie | 110 | 6.00 | 4.80 |
  | Mango Fruit Tea | 80 | 5.50 | 1.30 |

- **Three-step method for asking AI about data:**
  1. **Explain the data:** Tell the AI what each column means, what unit it uses, and how to calculate the measure you care about.
  2. **Get a table, then check one row:** Ask the AI to show its results in a table. Check one row yourself with a calculator.
  3. **Ask for a decision with facts and guesses separated:** State your goal. Ask the AI to list what the data shows, what it is only guessing, and then its recommendation.

- **Lecture Content (Hour 1 - 50 Minutes):**
  - **Part 1: Four business words you need first (15 min):**

    Before you ask an AI to analyze sales, you must know what the numbers mean. Each word below uses one cup of Brown Sugar Milk Tea as the example.

    - **Price:** What one customer pays for one cup. Example: $5.00.

    - **Ingredient cost:** What the shop pays for the tea, milk, sugar, and cup used to make one cup. Example: $2.40.

    - **Revenue:** All the money customers paid. Revenue = cups sold x price. Example: 420 cups x $5.00 = $2,100.00. Revenue is not the money the shop keeps, because the shop still has to pay for ingredients.

    - **Gross profit:** The money left after paying for ingredients. Gross profit per cup = price - ingredient cost. Example: $5.00 - $2.40 = $2.60 per cup. Weekly gross profit = cups sold x gross profit per cup. Example: 420 x $2.60 = $1,092.00. Gross profit does not subtract rent or staff wages, so it is not the same as the shop's final profit.

  - **Part 2: Why the first AI answer was wrong (15 min):**

    "Sells the most" and "earns the most" are different questions. Compare two drinks from the table.

    | Drink | Cups sold | Gross profit per cup | Weekly gross profit |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | Mango Fruit Tea | 80 (fewest) | $4.20 (highest) | $336.00 |
    | Taro Smoothie | 110 | $1.20 (lowest) | $132.00 (lowest) |

    - Mango Fruit Tea sells the fewest cups, but each cup earns $4.20.

    - Taro Smoothie sells more cups, but its ingredients are expensive, so each cup earns only $1.20.

    - If Mina removes Mango Fruit Tea, she loses $336.00 of gross profit per week. If she removes Taro Smoothie, she loses only $132.00.

    When a question gives no rule for deciding, an AI tool often uses the easiest number to see. Here, that was cups sold. The AI was not careless. The question was incomplete.

  - **Part 3: How the three-step method fixes the question (20 min):**

    The instructor runs Poor Prompt 1 and Improved Prompts 1 and 2 below in front of the class and compares the answers.

    - **Step 1 fixes the definition problem:** The AI now knows that "profit" means gross profit and how to calculate it.

    - **Step 2 fixes the trust problem:** A table with one shown calculation lets you check the AI's math in one minute. AI tools can make arithmetic mistakes, so always check at least one row.

    - **Step 3 fixes the decision problem:** The AI now knows the goal (a shorter line with the least profit lost). It must also say what it is guessing. For example, the table does not show how long each drink takes to make, so any claim about preparation time is a guess.

- **Prompt examples for the same bubble tea case:**

  > **Poor prompt 1**
  >
  > ```text

  > Which drink should I remove from the menu?

  > drink,cups_sold,price_per_cup_USD,ingredient_cost_per_cup_USD

  > Classic Milk Tea,380,4.50,1.60
  > Matcha Latte,160,5.50,2.10
  > Taro Smoothie,110,6.00,4.80
  > Mango Fruit Tea,80,5.50,1.30
  > ```
  >
  > **What the AI often answers:** "Remove Mango Fruit Tea because it has the lowest sales."
  >
  > **Why it is weak:**
  > - It gives no rule for deciding, so the AI uses the most visible number: cups sold.
  > - It does not state the goal: a shorter lunch line while keeping as much profit as possible.
  > - The result is a confident answer that removes the drink with the highest profit per cup.

  > **Poor prompt 2**
  >
  > ```text

  >
  > drink,cups_sold,price_per_cup_USD,ingredient_cost_per_cup_USD
  > Brown Sugar Milk Tea,420,5.00,2.40
  > Classic Milk Tea,380,4.50,1.60
  > Matcha Latte,160,5.50,2.10
  > Taro Smoothie,110,6.00,4.80
  > Mango Fruit Tea,80,5.50,1.30
  > ```
  >
  > **What the AI often answers:** Some answers call revenue "profit." Other answers invent rent or wage costs that are not in the data.
  >
  > **Why it is weak:**
  > - "Profit" has several meanings. This data can only support gross profit, and the prompt does not say so.
  > - Asking for an "exact answer" does not make the answer correct. It only makes the AI sound more certain.
  > - The prompt does not ask the AI to show its calculation, so you cannot check it.

  > **Improved prompt 1 (Steps 1 and 2)**
  >
  > ```text
  > I run a small bubble tea shop. Below is one week of sales data.
  >

  > - cups_sold: number of cups sold this week
  > - price_per_cup_USD: what one customer pays for one cup, in US dollars
  > - ingredient_cost_per_cup_USD: cost of the ingredients for one cup, in US dollars
  >

  > - Revenue = cups_sold x price_per_cup_USD
  > - Gross profit per cup = price_per_cup_USD - ingredient_cost_per_cup_USD
  > - Weekly gross profit = cups_sold x gross profit per cup
  > Gross profit does not include rent or staff wages.
  >

  > gross profit per cup, weekly gross profit.

  > Show the calculation for the Taro Smoothie row so I can check it.
  > Do not recommend anything yet.
  >
  > drink,cups_sold,price_per_cup_USD,ingredient_cost_per_cup_USD
  > Brown Sugar Milk Tea,420,5.00,2.40
  > Classic Milk Tea,380,4.50,1.60
  > Matcha Latte,160,5.50,2.10
  > Taro Smoothie,110,6.00,4.80
  > Mango Fruit Tea,80,5.50,1.30
  > ```
  >
  > **Why it works:**
  > - It explains each column and its unit, so the AI cannot misread the data.
  > - It gives the formula for gross profit, which removes the confusion in Poor Prompt 2.
  > - It asks for a sorted table, so the five drinks are easy to compare.
  > - It asks to see one calculation, so you can check the math.
  > - "Do not recommend anything yet" keeps calculating and deciding as separate steps.

  > **Improved prompt 2 (Step 3, sent in the same chat after Improved Prompt 1)**
  >
  > ```text
  > I checked your table with a calculator. Weekly gross profit is:
  > Brown Sugar Milk Tea $1,092.00, Classic Milk Tea $1,102.00,
  > Matcha Latte $544.00, Taro Smoothie $132.00, Mango Fruit Tea $336.00.
  >
  > My goal: remove one drink to make the lunch line shorter,
  > while losing as little weekly gross profit as possible.
  >
  > Answer in three parts:
  > 1. Facts: what the table shows. Use only the numbers above.
  > 2. Guesses: what you are assuming that the data does not show.
  > 3. Recommendation: which drink to remove, in one sentence, and why.
  > Then explain in two sentences why "remove the drink with the lowest
  > sales" is a weak rule for this shop.
  > ```
  >
  > **Why it works:**
  > - It gives the checked numbers, so the AI does not recalculate and make a new mistake.
  > - It states the goal and the decision rule: shorter line, least gross profit lost.
  > - Separating facts from guesses stops the AI from presenting unknown details, such as preparation time, as facts.
  > - The last request makes the AI explain the mistake in Poor Prompt 1, which checks the main lesson.

#### Session 2.2: Improve AI Results by Testing One Change

<a id="session-22-quality-control--improving-ai-results"></a>

![Illustration: Leo, the owner of a small online phone accessory shop, sits at a laptop. The inbox shows "20 return emails today." A policy card lists four rules: unopened with receipt within 14 days is a refund; damaged and reported within 7 days is a replacement; opened and not damaged is not eligible; missing receipt means asking for the order number. Leo asks, "Does this AI reply follow our return policy?" in a minimal Korean layered-hanji paper-cut style.](images/session_2_2_return_policy_hanji.png)

* **Lecture Presentation:** [Download Slides (PDF)](slides/session_2_2_lecture.pdf)

- **Duration:** 3 hours: 50-minute lesson, 10-minute break, 50-minute guided practice (Exercise 1), 10-minute break, 50-minute applied practice (Exercise 2), and 10-minute wrap-up.
- **Problem:** Leo runs a small online shop that sells phone accessories. His root problem is time: he receives about 20 return emails a day, and answering them takes about two hours. Leo's idea is to let ChatGPT draft the replies. He pasted four emails and asked, "Please reply to these customer emails." The replies were friendly, but they broke the shop's return policy. ChatGPT promised a refund to a customer who had opened and used the item, and to a customer who had no receipt. Those two promises are worth $70. The AI did not know the policy because the prompt did not include it.
- **Goal:** Learn how to improve an AI answer step by step: give the AI the rules, check every answer against the rules, and change one instruction at a time until the answers are correct.
- **Practice data:** [Download the return emails CSV](data/session_2_2_return_emails.csv). This is fictional data created for this class.

  **Return policy (Leo's shop)**

  | Rule | Situation | Result |
  |---|---|---|
  | 1 | Unopened item, with receipt, returned within 14 days of delivery | Refund |
  | 2 | Item reported damaged within 7 days of delivery | Replacement |
  | 3 | Opened item that is not damaged | Not eligible for a refund |
  | 4 | Receipt or a needed detail is missing | Information needed: ask for the order number and do not promise a refund |

  **Customer emails**

  | Case | Item | Price (USD) | Email |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | A | Phone case | 25.00 | I bought a phone case 10 days ago. It is still in the box, and I have my receipt. Can I return it? |
  | B | Screen protector | 15.00 | My screen protector arrived cracked yesterday. I can send a photo. What can you do? |
  | C | Wireless earbuds | 40.00 | I got the wireless earbuds 6 days ago. I opened them and used them. They work fine, but I changed my mind. Please refund me. |
  | D | Charging cable | 30.00 | I want a refund for my charging cable, but I lost the receipt. |

- **Three-step method for improving an AI answer:**
  1. **Give the AI the rules:** Paste the policy and tell the AI to use only that policy.
  2. **Check every answer against the rules:** Compare each AI result with the answer key and write down one error.
  3. **Change one instruction, then run it again:** Fix only that error, run the same emails again, and record the change in a change log.

- **Lecture Content (Hour 1 - 50 Minutes):**
  - **Part 1: Five words you need first (15 min):**

    Before you ask an AI to answer customers, you must know what the policy words mean. Each example uses a delivery on March 1.

    - **Return policy:** The shop's written rules about returns. The AI must follow these rules, not its own idea of what is fair.

    - **Refund:** The customer gets the money back. Example: a $25.00 phone case is returned, and the customer gets $25.00.

    - **Replacement:** The customer gets a new item instead of money. Example: a cracked screen protector is replaced with a new one.

    - **Return window:** The number of days a customer has to ask. Example: for a delivery on March 1, the 14-day refund window ends on March 15, and the 7-day damage window ends on March 8.

    - **Order number:** The code that identifies a purchase, such as #4471. When a receipt is missing, the shop uses the order number to check the purchase before it decides.

  - **Part 2: Why the friendly AI reply was wrong (15 min):**

    "Polite" and "correct" are different questions. Compare what the AI promised with what the policy says.

    | Case | What the AI promised | What the policy says | Rule |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | A | Refund | Refund | 1 |
    | B | Refund | Replacement | 2 |
    | C | Refund ($40.00) | Not eligible for a refund | 3 |
    | D | Refund ($30.00) | Information needed: ask for the order number | 4 |

    - Only Case A is correct. B, C, and D are wrong.

    - Cases C and D are refunds the shop should not promise. Together they are worth $40.00 + $30.00 = $70.00.

    - The AI was not careless. Without the policy, it used a general idea of good service: "say yes to the customer."

  - **Part 3: How to improve the prompt one change at a time (20 min):**

    The instructor runs Poor Prompt 1 and Improved Prompts 1 and 2 below in front of the class and compares the answers.

    - **Why only one change:** If you change many instructions at once and the answer improves, you do not know which change helped. Change one instruction, run the same emails again, and compare.

    - **A change log:** A small table that records each test. It has three columns: the weakness you saw, the one instruction you changed, and the result.

      | Weakness | One change | Result |
      |---|---|---|
      | The AI promised refunds to C and D | Added the return policy and "Use only the policy below" | C became Not eligible; D became Information needed |

    - **Check before you send:** AI tools can still make mistakes after a good prompt. Always compare each result with the policy before a reply goes to a customer.

- **Prompt examples for the same online shop case:**

  > **Poor prompt 1**
  >
  > ```text
  > Please reply to these customer emails for my online shop.
  >
  > A: I bought a phone case 10 days ago. It is still in the box, and I have my receipt. Can I return it?
  > B: My screen protector arrived cracked yesterday. I can send a photo. What can you do?
  > C: I got the wireless earbuds 6 days ago. I opened them and used them. They work fine, but I changed my mind. Please refund me.
  > D: I want a refund for my charging cable, but I lost the receipt.
  > ```
  >
  > **What the AI often answers:** Four friendly replies that all offer a refund, for example: "We're sorry the earbuds didn't work out for you. We'll be happy to process your refund."
  >
  > **Why it is weak:**
  > - It does not include the return policy, so the AI uses a general idea of good service instead of the shop's rules.
  > - It does not ask the AI to decide a result for each case before writing, so the decision is hidden inside polite sentences.
  > - The result is a set of promises that break the policy and could cost the shop $70.00.

  > **Poor prompt 2**
  >
  > ```text
  > Here is our return policy:
  > 1. Unopened item, with receipt, returned within 14 days of delivery: Refund.
  > 2. Item reported damaged within 7 days of delivery: Replacement.
  > 3. Opened item that is not damaged: Not eligible for a refund.
  > 4. Receipt or a needed detail is missing: ask for the order number. Do not promise a refund.
  >
  > Reply to these customer emails. Make every customer happy and say yes to every request.
  >
  > A: I bought a phone case 10 days ago. It is still in the box, and I have my receipt. Can I return it?
  > B: My screen protector arrived cracked yesterday. I can send a photo. What can you do?
  > C: I got the wireless earbuds 6 days ago. I opened them and used them. They work fine, but I changed my mind. Please refund me.
  > D: I want a refund for my charging cable, but I lost the receipt.
  > ```
  >
  > **What the AI often answers:** Replies that mention the policy but still approve Case C "as a one-time exception" and promise Case D a refund "once we find your order."
  >
  > **Why it is weak:**
  > - "Say yes to every request" conflicts with the policy, and the AI often follows the last instruction.
  > - It does not say which rule wins when the customer's request and the policy disagree.
  > - It still asks for replies only, so you cannot quickly see whether each decision follows a rule.

  > **Improved prompt 1 (Steps 1 and 2)**
  >
  > ```text
  > I run a small online shop that sells phone accessories.
  > Use only the return policy below to decide four customer emails.
  >
  > Return policy:
  > 1. Unopened item, with receipt, returned within 14 days of delivery: Refund.
  > 2. Item reported damaged within 7 days of delivery: Replacement.
  > 3. Opened item that is not damaged: Not eligible for a refund.
  > 4. Receipt or a needed detail is missing: Information needed.
  >    Ask for the order number. Do not promise a refund.
  >
  > Make a table with these columns: case, result, rule number, one-sentence reason.
  > The result must be exactly one of: Refund, Replacement, Not eligible, Information needed.
  > Do not invent exceptions. Do not write the replies yet.
  >
  > A: I bought a phone case 10 days ago. It is still in the box, and I have my receipt. Can I return it?
  > B: My screen protector arrived cracked yesterday. I can send a photo. What can you do?
  > C: I got the wireless earbuds 6 days ago. I opened them and used them. They work fine, but I changed my mind. Please refund me.
  > D: I want a refund for my charging cable, but I lost the receipt.
  > ```
  >
  > **Why it works:**
  > - It gives the AI the policy and says "use only" this policy, which fixes the problem in Poor Prompt 1.
  > - It limits the result to four labels, so every decision is easy to compare with the answer key.
  > - It asks for the rule number, so you can see which rule the AI used for each case.
  > - "Do not invent exceptions" removes the pressure to say yes that caused the problem in Poor Prompt 2.
  > - "Do not write the replies yet" keeps deciding and writing as separate steps.

  > **Improved prompt 2 (Step 3, sent in the same chat after Improved Prompt 1)**
  >
  > ```text
  > I checked your table against our policy. The results are correct:
  > A Refund, B Replacement, C Not eligible, D Information needed.
  >
  > Now write one reply for each customer. Rules for every reply:
  > - Say the result in the first sentence.
  > - Give the reason from the policy in one sentence.
  > - Tell the customer the next step.
  > - If information is missing, ask for it and do not promise a refund.
  > - Be polite. Keep each reply under 60 words.
  > ```
  >
  > **Why it works:**
  > - It gives the AI the checked results, so the replies cannot change the decisions.
  > - It sets the order of each reply (result, reason, next step), so customers get a clear answer.
  > - It keeps the "do not promise" rule for Case D, the case where the AI made a costly mistake.
  > - "Be polite" is still there, so the replies stay friendly without breaking the policy.

<a id="session-23-scoping-projects--setting-goals"></a>

- **Problem:** A student club wants to open a coffee cart on campus. It has $4,000, eight weeks, and three volunteers. - **Three-step method for planning a project with AI:**
  1. **State the boundary:** Tell the AI the outcome, deadline, budget, team size, and what version 1 includes.
  2. **Sort and check:** Ask the AI to sort the work into in scope, out of scope, and to be confirmed (TBD). Check the budget total yourself.
  3. **Test every change request:** Before saying yes to a new idea, check its cost, time, and approvals against the boundary.

- **Lecture Content (Hour 1 - 50 Minutes):**
  - **Part 1: Five project words you need first (15 min):**

    Before you ask an AI to plan a project, you must know these words. Each example uses the coffee cart.

    - **Scope:** The work included in this version of the project. In scope: drip coffee and tea. Out of scope: work saved for a later version, such as an espresso machine.

    - **Budget and buffer:** The budget is the most money the project can spend. The buffer is the money left after the planned costs. Example: $4,000 - $3,200 = $800 buffer.

    - **Milestone:** A checkable result by a certain week. Example: "Week 2: food permit application submitted." You can answer yes or no.

    - **Dependency:** Something the team needs from another person or office before work can continue. Example: the cart cannot open until the campus approves the space.

    - **TBD (to be determined):** A fact the team still needs to confirm. Example: the approval time is TBD. TBD means "find out," not "guess."

  - **Part 2: Why the AI plan was wrong (15 min):**

    "Looks complete" and "fits the project" are different questions. Compare the AI plan with the project facts.

    | AI plan said | Project facts | Problem |
    |---|---|---|
    | Buy an espresso machine | Version 1 menu is drip coffee and tea | Out of scope, +$2,500.00 |
    | Open in week 4 | Target is week 8; approval time is unknown | Invented date |
    | "Campus approval: week 1" | Applications are not filed yet | Unknown shown as fact |

    - Version 1 quotes: $3,200.00. With the espresso machine: $3,200.00 + $2,500.00 = $5,700.00.

    - $5,700.00 - $4,000.00 = $1,700.00 over budget.

    - The AI was not careless. Without the limits, it planned the "best" coffee cart instead of the one the club can afford.

  - **Part 3: How the three-step method fixes the plan (20 min):**

    The instructor runs Poor Prompt 1 and Improved Prompts 1 and 2 below in front of the class and compares the answers.

    - **Step 1 fixes the boundary problem:** The AI now knows the budget, deadline, team, and version 1 menu.

    - **Step 2 fixes the trust problem:** A sorted cost table with a total lets you check the AI's math in one minute. AI tools can add numbers wrong, so always check the total.

    - **Step 3 fixes the "yes to everything" problem:** Each new idea is checked for cost, time, and approvals before the team accepts it. Unknowns stay TBD.

- **Prompt examples for the same coffee cart case:**

  > **Poor prompt 1**
  >
  > ```text
  > We are a student club. Write a complete plan for opening a coffee cart on campus.
  > ```
  >
  > **What the AI often answers:** A long, professional plan with an espresso machine, a large menu, a marketing campaign, and an opening date in about four weeks.
  >
  > **Why it is weak:**
  > - It gives no budget, deadline, team size, or menu, so the AI plans an ideal cart instead of this club's cart.
  > - It does not ask the AI to mark unknown facts, so the AI fills them with guesses, such as an approval date.
  > - The result looks finished, but it is $1,700.00 over budget when you use the club's real quotes.

  > **Poor prompt 2**
  >
  > ```text

  >
  > **What the AI often answers:** A plan that keeps every idea, says the cart "will open in week 8," and gives no total cost.
  >
  > **Why it is weak:**
  > - "Include every idea" removes the boundary, so nothing is out of scope.
  > - "Guarantee" asks for certainty that no one has, because the approval time is unknown.
  > - It gives no prices, so neither the AI nor the team can check whether the plan fits $4,000.

  > **Improved prompt 1 (Steps 1 and 2)**
  >
  > ```text
  > Our student club plans a coffee cart on campus.
  > Project facts:
  > - Budget: $4,000 maximum. Target: open in 8 weeks. Team: 3 volunteers.
  > - Version 1 menu: drip coffee and tea only.
  > - Campus space approval and a food permit are required.
  >   The applications are not filed yet. The approval time is unknown.
  >
  > Version 1 price quotes (USD):
  > Used coffee cart 1,200; Two drip coffee brewers 300; Hot water dispenser 250;
  > Cups, lids, and sleeves 400; Coffee beans and tea 600; Signs and menu board 150;
  > Campus food permit fee 200; Card reader 100.
  > Other ideas: Espresso machine 2,500; Fresh juice 1,250; Mobile ordering app (no quote).
  >

  > ```
  >
  > **Why it works:**
  > - It gives the budget, deadline, team, and version 1 menu, which fixes the missing boundary in Poor Prompt 1.
  > - It gives the club's own quotes, so the AI does not invent prices.
  > - The three lists make every idea visible as in scope, out of scope, or TBD.
  > - It asks for a total and a buffer, so you can check the budget with a calculator.
  > - "Do not write the plan yet" keeps sorting and planning as separate steps.

  > **Improved prompt 2 (Step 3, sent in the same chat after Improved Prompt 1)**
  >
  > ```text
  > I checked your table. The version 1 total is $3,200, and the buffer is $800.

  > - Included work and excluded work
  > - Four milestones, each with a target week and a yes/no check

  > ```
  >
  > **Why it works:**
  > - It gives the checked total, so the outline cannot quietly go over budget.
  > - Milestones with a target week and a yes/no check make progress easy to measure.
  > - Asking for one dependency and one risk shows what could delay the cart.
  > - "Mark every unknown date as TBD" stops the AI from inventing an approval date.

  | Note | What the person said |

  | N1 | My work shifts change every week, so my pay changes too. I want to know how much I can spend before Friday. |

- **Three-step method for building profiles with AI:**
  1. **Give the AI the evidence:** Paste the interview notes and the product facts. Tell the AI to use only them.
  2. **Require a note for every detail:** Ask the AI to cite a note ID for each need and barrier. Check each citation yourself.
  3. **Check every message against the product:** A message may use only available features. Label anything untested as a hypothesis.

- **Lecture Content (Hour 1 - 50 Minutes):**
  - **Part 1: Five words you need first (15 min):**

    Before you ask an AI to describe customers, you must know these words. Each example uses the budgeting app.

    - **Evidence:** What real people said or did. In this session, the evidence is the six interview notes, N1 to N6.

    - **Need:** The progress a person wants to make. Example (N1): "I want to know how much I can spend before Friday."

    - **Barrier:** What stops a person from trying a product. Example (N6): "I do not want a new app to connect to my bank account."

    - **Customer profile:** A short summary of one group of people with the same need, supported by at least two notes. Example: "People whose pay changes and who want to know safe weekly spending (N1, N2)."

    - **Hypothesis:** A claim the team believes but has not tested yet. Example: "A weekly spending view will help people with changing pay." It must be labeled as a hypothesis, not stated as a fact.

  - **Part 2: Why the AI persona was not useful (15 min):**

    "Sounds like a real person" and "comes from evidence" are different questions. Check each detail of the AI persona against the notes.

    | AI persona detail | Supporting note | Result |
    |---|---|---|
    | Sarah, 24 | None | Invented |

    - 0 of 5 details come from the interviews. One detail goes against the evidence.

    - The ad promised bank syncing. A customer who downloads the app for that feature will be disappointed, and the claim is false.

    - The AI was not careless. Without notes, it filled the profile with typical marketing details.

  - **Part 3: How the three-step method fixes the profile (20 min):**

    The instructor runs Poor Prompt 1 and Improved Prompts 1 and 2 below in front of the class and compares the answers.

    - **Step 1 fixes the evidence problem:** The AI can only use what people actually said.

    - **Step 2 fixes the trust problem:** Every need and barrier has a note ID, so you can check each one in a few seconds. A detail with no note is removed or turned into a question for the next interview.

    - **Step 3 fixes the false-promise problem:** Each message names the feature it uses. A feature that the app does not have cannot appear.

#### Session 3.1: Financial Analysis & Executive Briefs

<a id="session-31-financial-analysis--executive-briefs"></a>

![Illustration: Two shop managers look at a table with Year A revenue $20,000, COGS $12,000, OPEX $5,000, gross profit $8,000, and gross margin 40%, and Year B revenue $22,000, COGS $14,300, OPEX $5,200, gross profit $7,700, and gross margin 35%. One asks, "Why did sales rise while gross profit fell?" in a minimal Korean layered-hanji paper-cut style.](images/session_3_1_financial_analysis_hanji.png)

* **Lecture Presentation:** [Download Slides (PDF)](slides/session_3_1_lecture.pdf)

- **Duration:** 3 hours: 50-minute lesson, 10-minute break, 50-minute guided practice (Exercise 1), 10-minute break, 50-minute applied practice (Exercise 2), and 10-minute wrap-up.
- **Problem:** Hana runs a small tea and snack shop. Her sales grew from $20,000 to $22,000, a 10% increase. But at the end of the year, she has less money left than before. Her root problem is that she does not know why, and she is about to hire a part-time worker. Her idea is to ask ChatGPT. She asked, "My sales went up 10%. Is my shop doing better? Should I hire?" ChatGPT said, "Yes. 10% growth is strong. Hiring will help you grow." But the shop's profit actually fell. The AI looked only at sales because the prompt did not include the costs.
- **Goal:** Learn how to use AI to read a simple income statement: give the AI the numbers and the formulas, check the results yourself, and ask for a short brief that separates facts from guesses.
- **Practice data:** [Download the shop results CSV](data/session_3_1_shop_results.csv). This is fictional data created for this class.

  **Hana's shop results (USD)**

  | Year | Revenue | Cost of goods sold (COGS) | Operating expenses (OPEX) |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Year A | 20,000 | 12,000 | 5,000 |
  | Year B | 22,000 | 14,300 | 5,200 |

- **Three-step method for reading financial results with AI:**
  1. **Give the AI the numbers and the formulas:** Paste the data and define each measure, so the AI calculates the right thing.
  2. **Get a table, then check two cells:** Ask for one table with all measures. Check at least two cells with a calculator.
  3. **Ask for a brief with facts and guesses separated:** Ask for a short brief that states the facts, labels any explanation as "Not verified," and asks one question.

- **Lecture Content (Hour 1 - 50 Minutes):**
  - **Part 1: Six money words you need first (15 min):**

    Before you ask an AI about profit, you must know what each number means. Each example uses Year A.

    - **Revenue:** All the money customers paid. Year A: $20,000.

    - **Cost of goods sold (COGS):** What the shop paid for the tea and snacks it sold. Year A: $12,000.

    - **Gross profit:** Revenue minus COGS. Year A: $20,000 - $12,000 = $8,000.

    - **Gross margin:** Gross profit as a percent of revenue. Year A: $8,000 / $20,000 = 40%. It shows how much of each dollar of sales is left after paying for the goods.

    - **Operating expenses (OPEX):** The other costs of running the shop, such as rent and wages. Year A: $5,000.

    - **Operating profit:** Gross profit minus OPEX. Year A: $8,000 - $5,000 = $3,000. This is closer to the money the shop really keeps.

  - **Part 2: Why the AI answer was wrong (15 min):**

    "Sales went up" and "the shop earns more" are different questions. Compare the two years.

    | Measure | Year A | Year B | Change |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | Revenue | $20,000 | $22,000 | +$2,000 (+10%) |
    | Gross profit | $8,000 | $7,700 | -$300 |
    | Gross margin | 40% | 35% | -5 percentage points |
    | Operating profit | $3,000 | $2,500 | -$500 |

    - Sales went up by 10%, but operating profit went down by $500.

    - COGS grew faster than revenue, so each dollar of sales left less money: 40 cents in Year A and 35 cents in Year B.

    - A percentage point is the simple difference between two percents: 40% - 35% = 5 percentage points.

  - **Part 3: How the three-step method fixes the answer (20 min):**

    The instructor runs Poor Prompt 1 and Improved Prompts 1 and 2 below in front of the class and compares the answers.

    - **Step 1 fixes the missing-cost problem:** With COGS, OPEX, and the formulas, the AI can see that profit fell.

    - **Step 2 fixes the trust problem:** AI tools can make arithmetic mistakes, especially with percents. Checking two cells takes one minute.

    - **Step 3 fixes the guessing problem:** The data shows that profit fell, not why it fell. Higher supplier prices or more discounts are possible explanations, so they must be labeled "Not verified."

#### Session 3.2: Operations & Process Improvement

- **Goal:** Learn how to use AI to study a process problem: give the AI the process and the log, separate evidence from guesses, and plan a small test before spending money.
- **Practice data:** [Download the order log CSV](data/session_3_2_order_log.csv). This is fictional data created for this class.

  **The order process**

  | This week | Tue | 29 | 12 | 0 of 2 | No |

- **Three-step method for studying a process problem with AI:**
  1. **Give the AI the process and the log:** Paste the steps and the data. Tell the AI to use only them.
  2. **Separate evidence from guesses:** Ask for late rates, the evidence, the unknowns, and possible causes in separate lists. Check the rates yourself.

- **Lecture Content (Hour 1 - 50 Minutes):**
  - **Part 1: Five process words you need first (15 min):**

    Before you ask an AI about a process problem, you must know these words. Each example uses Sora's warehouse.

    - **Process step:** One action in the work. The warehouse has four steps: Pick, Scan, Pack, and Courier.

    - **Late rate:** Late orders as a percent of all orders. This Monday: 18 / 30 = 60%.

    - **Evidence:** A fact in the log. Example: "This week, 0 of 2 scanners were charged at opening."

    - **Possible cause:** An explanation that fits the evidence but is not proven. Example: "Uncharged scanners slowed the Scan step."

    - **Small test:** A short, low-cost check of one possible cause. Example: sign the charging checklist every day for one week and compare the late rate.

    "Sounds like a solution" and "fits the evidence" are different questions. Compare the AI answer with Sora's log.

    | What the AI said | What the log shows | Problem |
    |---|---|---|
    | Staff are too slow | No timing data by step | A guess about people |
    | Hire two more pickers | Orders per day are the same as last week (about 30) | Not supported |
    | Buy new scanners for $1,200 | The scanners work; they were not charged | An expensive fix for a guessed cause |

    - Last week, 10 of 150 orders were late: 6.7%. This week, 44 of 90 orders were late: 48.9%.

    - The big change this week is that the scanners were not charged and the checklist was not signed.

    - This pattern points to a possible cause. It does not prove it. Something else may also have changed this week.

  - **Part 3: How the three-step method fixes the answer (20 min):**

    The instructor runs Poor Prompt 1 and Improved Prompts 1 and 2 below in front of the class and compares the answers.

    - **Step 1 fixes the missing-evidence problem:** With the process and the log, the AI can compare this week with last week.

    - **Step 2 fixes the blame problem:** Separate lists keep facts, unknowns, and guesses apart, so no worker is blamed without evidence.

    - **Step 3 fixes the expensive-fix problem:** A one-week checklist test costs almost nothing. Sora should run it before spending $1,200.

  > ```

  > - It gives no process and no log, so the AI uses general reasons for late orders.

  > - The result blames workers and suggests spending money on a guessed cause.

    - **Requirement:** A skill the person must have to do a duty. Example: clear writing, because the person answers emails.

    - **Job-related:** Connected to a duty. Age, accent, and "energy" are not job-related, so they must not be requirements.

    - **Structured interview:** Every candidate gets the same questions in the same order, so the answers can be compared fairly.

#### Session 3.4: Workplace AI Ethics, Privacy & Bias

<a id="session-34-workplace-ai-ethics-privacy--bias"></a>

![Illustration: A support employee holds a customer message about an issue with a recent invoice. The name, phone, account ID, and invoice ID are blacked out. - **Problem:** Minho works in customer support at an online shop. His root problem is time: he answers about 50 customer messages a day. His idea is to let AI draft the replies. He pasted a full customer message into a free AI chat tool: "Hi, this is Daniel Lee (account 84721). I'm having an issue with my recent invoice 3029. I think I was charged twice. The AI reply also repeated the account number and promised, "We have refunded the double charge," which no one had checked. Minho broke two company rules because he did not check what the message contained before he pasted it.
- **Goal:** Learn how to use AI safely with customer messages: check the company rules, remove personal details yourself before using AI, and review every AI draft before it is sent.
- **Practice data:** [Download the company AI rules CSV](data/session_3_4_ai_rules.csv). This is fictional data created for this class. Never use real customer data in this class.

  **Company AI rules**

  | Rule | Text |
  |---|---|
  | 1 | Use AI only through the company's approved AI tool. |
  | 2 | Never paste names, phone numbers, email addresses, account IDs, invoice or order numbers, or payment details into any AI tool. |
  | 3 | Replace personal details with placeholders such as [CUSTOMER] or [INVOICE]. |
  | 4 | A person must review every AI draft before it is sent. |

  **Customer message (fictional)**
  - "Hi, this is Daniel Lee (account 84721). I'm having an issue with my recent invoice 3029. I think I was charged twice. Please call me at 555-0184."

- **Three-step method for using AI safely with customer data:**
  1. **Check the rules first:** Is the AI tool approved? What must never be pasted?
  2. **Remove personal details yourself:** Mark every personal detail and replace it with a placeholder before anything goes into AI. Do not ask the AI to remove them for you.
  3. **Draft, then review:** Use AI only with the safe summary, then check the draft against a short review checklist before sending.

- **Lecture Content (Hour 1 - 50 Minutes):**
  - **Part 1: Five privacy words you need first (15 min):**

    Before you use AI with customer messages, you must know these words. Each example uses Daniel Lee's message.

    - **Personal data:** Any information about a specific person. Example: a name, a phone number, or a health condition.

    - **Identifier:** A detail that points to one person or one account. Example: account 84721 or invoice 3029.

    - **Data minimization:** Use only the information the task needs. To draft a reply, the AI needs "charged twice," not the phone number.

    - **Placeholder:** A label that stands in for a removed detail. Example: [CUSTOMER] instead of "Daniel Lee." You add the real detail back only after the draft is reviewed.

    - **Approved tool:** An AI tool that your company has checked and allowed for work data. A free public chat tool is not approved unless the company says so.

  - **Part 2: Why pasting the full message was wrong (15 min):**

    "The AI can help" and "the AI may see this data" are different questions. Check what Minho pasted.

    | Detail in the message | Type | Needed to draft the reply? |
    |---|---|---|
    | Daniel Lee | Name | No: use [CUSTOMER] |
    | 84721 | Account ID | No: use [ACCOUNT] |
    | 3029 | Invoice ID | No: use [INVOICE] |
    | 555-0184 | Phone number | No: remove it |
    | "charged twice" | The problem | Yes |

    - 4 of the 5 details were personal identifiers. The reply needed only "charged twice."

    - Minho broke Rule 1 (the tool was not approved) and Rule 2 (he pasted personal details).

    - The AI also promised a refund that no one had checked. Rule 4 exists to catch this kind of mistake.

  - **Part 3: How the three-step method fixes the problem (20 min):**

    The instructor runs Poor Prompt 1 and Improved Prompts 1 and 2 below in front of the class and compares the answers.

    - **Step 1 fixes the tool problem:** Checking the rules first means work data goes only into an approved tool.

    - **Step 2 fixes the privacy problem:** The person removes the details before anything is pasted, so no personal data reaches the AI.

    - **Step 3 fixes the false-promise problem:** A short review checklist catches invented promises and leftover personal details before the customer sees them.

- **Prompt examples for the same customer support case:**

  > **Poor prompt 1**
  >
  > ```text
  > Write a reply to this customer:
  > Hi, this is Daniel Lee (account 84721). I'm having an issue with my recent invoice 3029.
  > I think I was charged twice. Please call me at 555-0184.
  > ```
  >
  > **What the AI often answers:** "Dear Daniel Lee, thank you for contacting us about invoice 3029 on account 84721. We have refunded the double charge and will call you at 555-0184."
  >
  > **Why it is weak:**
  > - It pastes four personal details into the AI, which breaks Rule 2.
  > - It does not say what the shop has checked, so the AI promises a refund that may be wrong.
  > - The reply repeats the account and phone number, so the private data spreads even further.

- **Problem:** Rina leads a small website team. Every week, someone spends about 30 minutes writing the meeting recap from scratch, and the recaps look different each time. Her root problem is that tasks get lost: last month, two tasks were missed because no one was named as the owner. Her idea is to let ChatGPT write the recap. She pasted the notes and asked, "Summarize these meeting notes." The summary was smooth, but it said, "Priya will handle the legal review by Friday." The notes do not name anyone for the legal review. The summary also left out the decision to postpone the pricing page. - **Goal:** Learn how to turn a weekly task into a reusable AI template: fix the output sections, add a rule for missing information, and reuse the same template by changing only the input.
- **Practice data:** [Download the meeting notes CSV](data/session_4_1_meeting_notes.csv). This is fictional data created for this class.

  **Meeting notes, Week 1**
  - "Website FAQ update: Jamie drafts by Friday. Priya reviews Monday. Team agreed to postpone the pricing page until legal review. No owner named for legal review. Next check-in Tuesday."

  **Time spent on recaps**
  - Writing a recap from scratch: about 30 minutes a week.
  - Checking a template recap against the notes: about 5 minutes a week.

- **Three-step method for building a reusable AI workflow:**
  1. **Fix the output:** Decide the same sections for every week, such as Decisions, Actions, Open questions, and Next meeting.
  2. **Add a rule for missing information:** Tell the AI to write "Not stated" when an owner or a date is not in the notes.

- **Lecture Content (Hour 1 - 50 Minutes):**
  - **Part 1: Five workflow words you need first (15 min):**

    Before you build a reusable prompt, you must know these words. Each example uses Rina's weekly recap.

    - **Recurring task:** Work that happens again and again in the same way. Example: the weekly meeting recap.

    - **Template:** A prompt you save and reuse. Only one part changes each time: the input.

    - **Placeholder:** A marked spot in the template where the new input goes. Example: {Meeting_Notes}.

    - **Output format:** The fixed sections the answer must have. Example: Decisions; Actions (Task, Owner, Due date); Open questions; Next meeting.

    - **"Not stated" rule:** An instruction for missing information. If the notes do not name an owner, the AI writes "Not stated" instead of guessing.

  - **Part 2: Why the AI summary was wrong (15 min):**

    "Reads well" and "matches the notes" are different questions. Compare the AI summary with the notes.

    | AI summary said | The notes say | Problem |
    |---|---|---|
    | "Priya will handle the legal review by Friday." | No owner named for legal review | Invented owner and date |
    | (nothing) | Postpone the pricing page until legal review | Decision left out |
    | "The meeting went well, and the team is aligned." | (nothing) | Filler that is not in the notes |

    - A wrong owner is worse than no owner: Priya may never know she was "assigned," and the task is lost again.

    - Saving time: writing from scratch takes 30 minutes a week, and checking a template recap takes about 5. That saves 25 minutes a week, or about 100 minutes in four weeks.

  - **Part 3: How the three-step method fixes the recap (20 min):**

    The instructor runs Poor Prompt 1 and Improved Prompts 1 and 2 below in front of the class and compares the answers.

    - **Step 3 fixes the start-from-scratch problem:** The same template every week gives the same format, and the team only needs to check it.

#### Session 4.2: Troubleshooting & Polishing Prompts

<a id="session-42-troubleshooting--polishing-prompts"></a>

![Illustration: A student holds a Social Campaign Brief for a reusable bottle aimed at local students. Budget, channel, and success measure all say "Not provided," and an AI robot waits nearby. * **Lecture Presentation:** [Download Slides (PDF)](slides/session_4_2_lecture.pdf)

- **Duration:** 3 hours: 50-minute lesson, 10-minute break, 50-minute guided practice (Exercise 1), 10-minute break, 50-minute applied practice (Exercise 2), and 10-minute wrap-up.
- **Problem:** Joon is a marketing intern at a small shop that sells eco-friendly products. His manager asked for a two-week social media plan for a new reusable bottle. The brief did not include the budget, the channel, or the success measure. Joon's root problem is that he must deliver a plan quickly, but key facts are missing. His idea is to let ChatGPT write the plan. The plan looked ready to approve: "Budget: $5,000. Channel: influencer ads. Goal: 1,000 sales." All three numbers and choices were invented. The real budget, which the manager gave later, was $300. The AI filled the gaps because the prompt never told it what to do when information is missing.
- **Goal:** Learn how to test and fix a prompt: run it with complete and incomplete inputs, find where it invents information, and add one fallback instruction until it handles missing facts safely.
- **Practice data:** [Download the campaign brief CSV](data/session_4_2_campaign_brief.csv). This is fictional data created for this class.

  **Campaign brief**

  | Field | Value |
  |---|---|
  | Product | Reusable water bottle, $18 each |
  | Audience | Local university students |
  | Timing | Two weeks |
  | Budget | Not provided |
  | Channel | Not provided |
  | Success measure | Not provided |

  **Test inputs for checking the prompt**

  | Test | Budget | Channel | Success measure |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Test 1 | Not provided | Not provided | Not provided |
  | Test 2 | $300 | Not provided | 100 visits to the product page |
  | Test 3 | $300 | The shop's Instagram account | Not provided |

- **Three-step method for troubleshooting a prompt:**
  1. **Test with different inputs:** Run the prompt with a complete brief and with briefs that miss information.
  2. **Find the bug:** Count the details in the answer that are not in the input. Each invented detail is a bug.
  3. **Patch and retest:** Add one fallback instruction for missing information, then run all the tests again.

- **Lecture Content (Hour 1 - 50 Minutes):**
  - **Part 1: Five testing words you need first (15 min):**

    Before you fix a prompt, you must know these words. Each example uses Joon's campaign brief.

    - **Test input:** One version of the brief that you use to try the prompt. Example: Test 2 has a budget but no channel.

    - **Bug:** A repeated wrong behavior in the answer. Example: the AI invents a budget whenever the budget is missing.

    - **Fallback instruction:** A rule for what the AI must do when information is missing. Example: "If the budget is missing, write 'Missing: budget' and ask for it."

    - **Patch:** A small change to the prompt that fixes one bug. Change one instruction at a time, so you know what helped.

    - **Retest:** Run all the test inputs again after a patch. A fix for one input must not break another.

  - **Part 2: Why the AI plan was a problem (15 min):**

    "Looks ready to approve" and "uses only the facts" are different questions. Compare the AI plan with the brief.

    | AI plan said | The brief says | Problem |
    |---|---|---|
    | Budget: $5,000 | Not provided | Invented number |
    | Channel: influencer ads | Not provided | Invented choice |
    | Goal: 1,000 sales | Not provided | Invented target |

    - 3 of 3 missing fields were filled with invented details, and none was marked as a guess.

    - The invented budget was more than 16 times the real budget: $5,000 / $300 = 16.7.

    - The AI was not careless. Without a fallback instruction, it treats "missing" as "make a reasonable guess."

  - **Part 3: How the three-step method fixes the prompt (20 min):**

    The instructor runs Poor Prompt 1 and Improved Prompts 1 and 2 below in front of the class and compares the answers.

    - **Step 1 fixes the hidden-bug problem:** A prompt that works on one complete brief may fail on an incomplete one. Testing several inputs shows the bug.

    - **Step 2 fixes the "looks fine" problem:** Counting invented details turns a feeling into a number you can compare after the patch.

    - **Step 3 fixes the bug safely:** One fallback instruction, then a retest of all inputs, shows whether the patch worked without breaking anything.

- **Prompt examples for the same campaign case:**

  > **Poor prompt 1**
  >
  > ```text
  > Plan a two-week social media campaign for our new reusable bottle.
  > We want more local students to learn about it.
  > ```
  >
  > **What the AI often answers:** A complete-looking plan with a budget of several thousand dollars, influencer ads, daily posts, and a sales target.
  >
  > **Why it is weak:**
  > - It does not list which facts are known and which are missing.
  > - It has no fallback instruction, so the AI fills every gap with a guess.
  > - The guesses look like facts, so a manager could approve the wrong budget.

  > **Poor prompt 2**
  >
  > ```text
  > Plan a two-week social media campaign for our new reusable bottle for local
  > university students. Make it complete and ready to approve.
  > Fill in any missing details with your best numbers.
  > ```
  >
  > **What the AI often answers:** A plan with an exact budget, channel, and sales goal, each presented as decided.
  >
  > **Why it is weak:**
  > - "Fill in missing details with your best numbers" tells the AI to invent facts.
  > - "Ready to approve" hides the fact that the team has not decided the budget or channel.
  > - Nothing in the answer shows which parts are real and which are guesses.

  > **Improved prompt 1 (Steps 1 and 2)**
  >
  > ```text
  > You help plan small social media campaigns. Use only the brief below.
  >
  > Brief:
  > - Product: reusable water bottle, $18 each
  > - Audience: local university students
  > - Timing: two weeks
  > - Budget: not provided
  > - Channel: not provided
  > - Success measure: not provided
  >

  > For each missing field, write "Missing: [field]" and ask one question about it.
  > Do not invent any budget, channel, goal, or number. Do not write the plan yet.
  > ```
  >
  > **Why it works:**
  > - It lists every field, so the AI can see exactly what is missing.
  > - "Mark it Provided or Missing" makes the gaps visible before any planning.
  > - "Missing: [field]" is the fallback instruction that fixes the bug in Poor Prompts 1 and 2.
  > - One question per missing field gives Joon a short list to ask his manager.
  > - "Do not write the plan yet" keeps checking and planning as separate steps.

  > **Improved prompt 2 (Step 3, sent in the same chat after Improved Prompt 1)**
  >
  > ```text
  > My manager answered two questions: the budget is $300, and the success
  > measure is 100 visits to the product page in two weeks.
  > The channel is still not decided.
  >

  > Show how the $300 is split; the total must be $300 or less.
  > Suggest two channel options and label each one "Option to approve."
  > Label any number you estimate as "Estimate."
  > ```
  >
  > **Why it works:**
  > - It adds only the facts the manager gave, so the plan uses real numbers.
  > - "The total must be $300 or less" gives a limit you can check with a calculator.
  > - "Option to approve" keeps the undecided channel as a choice, not a fact.
  > - The "Estimate" label shows which numbers are guesses.

  A good prompt must work on every test input. Use this template to run Test 2 and Test 3. Replace each part in square brackets.

  ```text

#### Session 4.3: Reviewing & Selecting Your Best Prompts

<a id="session-43-reviewing--selecting-your-best-prompts"></a>

![Illustration: A student compares three prompt cards: A, Daily support summary, estimated time saved per month 100 minutes; B, Weekly meeting recap, 40 minutes; and C, Quarterly report outline, 30 minutes. - **Duration:** 3 hours: 50-minute lesson, 10-minute break, 50-minute guided practice (Exercise 1), 10-minute break, 50-minute applied practice (Exercise 2), and 10-minute wrap-up.
- **Problem:** Ara works part-time in a small company's support office. She has written three useful prompts, but she has only two hours this week to test and polish one of them. Her root problem is choosing the prompt that will help most. Her idea is to ask ChatGPT. She asked, "Which of my three prompts is best to polish?" ChatGPT chose the quarterly report outline: "It is the most strategic and impressive." But that prompt is used once a month, saves the least time, and has the highest risk of number errors. The AI judged by how impressive the task sounds because the prompt gave it no facts and no criteria.
- **Goal:** Learn how to use AI to compare options with simple numbers: give the AI the facts and the formula, check the calculations, and decide with more than one criterion.
- **Practice data:** [Download the prompt candidates CSV](data/session_4_3_prompt_candidates.csv). This is fictional data created for this class.

  **Ara's three prompts**

  | Prompt | Task | Minutes per task without AI | Uses per month | Reuse ease (1-3) | Error risk (1-3) |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | A | Daily support summary | 10 | 20 | 3 | 1 |
  | B | Weekly meeting recap | 20 | 4 | 3 | 2 |
  | C | Quarterly report outline | 60 | 1 | 1 | 3 |

  **Estimate:** with a polished prompt, each task takes half the time.

- **Three-step method for choosing among options with AI:**
  1. **Give the AI the facts and the formula:** Paste the data and define how to calculate the value of each option.
  2. **Calculate, then check:** Ask for a table of results and check at least one row with a calculator.
  3. **Decide with more than one criterion:** Ask for a choice that considers time saved, reuse, and risk, and label every estimate.

- **Lecture Content (Hour 1 - 50 Minutes):**
  - **Part 1: Five decision words you need first (15 min):**

    Before you ask an AI to compare options, you must know these words. Each example uses Prompt A.

    - **Frequency:** How often a task happens. Prompt A: 20 times a month.

    - **Time saved per use:** Minutes saved each time. Prompt A: half of 10 minutes = 5 minutes.

    - **Time saved per month:** Time saved per use x uses per month. Prompt A: 5 x 20 = 100 minutes.

    - **Reuse ease and error risk:** Two scores from 1 to 3. Reuse ease 3 means the prompt works with little change. Error risk 3 means a mistake would be serious, such as a wrong number in a report.

    - **Estimate:** A number based on a guess, not a measurement. "Half the time" is an estimate, so the result must be labeled as an estimate.

  - **Part 2: Why the AI choice was weak (15 min):**

    "Sounds impressive" and "helps most" are different questions. Compare the three prompts.

    | Prompt | Time saved per use | Uses per month | Time saved per month | Reuse ease | Error risk |
    |---|---|---|---|---|---|
    | A | 5 min | 20 | 100 min | 3 | 1 |
    | B | 10 min | 4 | 40 min | 3 | 2 |
    | C | 30 min | 1 | 30 min | 1 | 3 |

    - The AI chose C, but C saves the least time per month: 30 minutes.

    - A saves the most time (100 minutes), is the easiest to reuse, and has the lowest risk.

    - C saves the most time per use, but it is used only once a month. A small saving that happens often can beat a big saving that happens rarely.

  - **Part 3: How the three-step method fixes the choice (20 min):**

    The instructor runs Poor Prompt 1 and Improved Prompts 1 and 2 below in front of the class and compares the answers.

    - **Step 1 fixes the missing-facts problem:** With frequency and minutes, the AI can calculate value instead of judging by the task name.

    - **Step 2 fixes the trust problem:** Checking one row with a calculator takes one minute, and multiplication mistakes are common.

    - **Step 3 fixes the one-number problem:** Time saved is important, but reuse and risk matter too. A fast prompt with a high risk still needs careful human checks.

- **Prompt examples for the same choice:**

  > **Poor prompt 1**
  >
  > ```text

  > ```

  >
  > **Why it is weak:**
  > - It asks for "impressive," which is not the same as useful.
  > - It still gives no numbers, so nothing can be checked.
  > - It ignores risk: a report full of numbers is where AI mistakes cost the most.

  > **Improved prompt 1 (Steps 1 and 2)**
  >
  > ```text
  > I want to choose one of my prompts to polish. Use only the facts below.
  >
  > Prompt A: daily support summary; 10 minutes per task without AI; used 20 times a month.

  > **Improved prompt 2 (Step 3, sent in the same chat after Improved Prompt 1)**
  >
  > ```text

  > - It gives the checked numbers, so the recommendation uses correct values.

Her root problem is that Marco has never used the template. She sent him only the prompt text in a chat message. Marco pasted the notes in the wrong place, used the prompt on a customer email, and sent a recap with a wrong date without checking it. - **Goal:** Learn how to document a tested prompt as a prompt card: fill in seven fixed fields, test the card with a new user, and include clear limits and human checks.
- **Practice data:** [Download the prompt card fields CSV](data/session_4_4_prompt_card_fields.csv). This is fictional data created for this class.

  **The tested prompt (from Session 4.1)**
  - "You turn meeting notes into a weekly recap. Use only the notes below. Write exactly these four sections: 1. Decisions 2. Actions, as a table with the columns Task | Owner | Due date 3. Open questions 4. Next meeting. If an owner or a date is not in the notes, write 'Not stated.' Do not add anything that is not in the notes. Notes: {Meeting_Notes}"

  **The seven fields of a prompt card**

  | Field | What it answers |
  |---|---|
  | Task | What problem does the prompt solve? |
  | Intended user | Who should use it? |
  | Input | What do I paste, and where? |
  | Prompt | The exact template to copy |
  | Output format | What will the answer look like? |
  | Do not use when | When must I not use it? |
  | Human checks | Three yes/no checks before I share the output |

- **Three-step method for documenting a prompt:**
  1. **Fill all seven fields:** Use the tested prompt and write each field in one or two short sentences.
  2. **Test the handoff:** Ask someone, or the AI acting as a new teammate, to read only the card and list what is unclear.
  3. **Fix and try it on new input:** Revise the card once, then run it on new input and use the human checks.

- **Lecture Content (Hour 1 - 50 Minutes):**
  - **Part 1: Five documentation words you need first (15 min):**

    Before you share a prompt, you must know these words. Each example uses Rina's recap template.

    - **Prompt card:** A short page that explains one tested prompt with fixed fields, so anyone can use it the same way.

    - **Handoff:** Giving your work to another person so they can do it without you. Example: Marco writes the recaps while Rina is away.

    - **Input instructions:** Exactly what to paste and where. Example: "Paste this week's meeting notes in place of {Meeting_Notes}."

    - **Do-not-use condition:** A situation where the prompt must not be used. Example: "Do not use for customer emails or any text with customer personal data."

    - **Human check:** A yes/no check a person does before sharing the output. Example: "Every name in the recap matches the notes."

  - **Part 2: Why the first handoff failed (15 min):**

    "Here is my prompt" and "here is how to use my prompt" are different things. Check what Rina sent to Marco.

    | Card field | In Rina's chat message? | What went wrong |
    |---|---|---|
    | Task | No | Marco did not know what the prompt was for |
    | Intended user | No | Not a problem this time |
    | Input | No | He pasted the notes in the wrong place |
    | Prompt | Yes | (sent) |
    | Output format | No | He did not know what a correct recap looks like |
    | Do not use when | No | He used it on a customer email |
    | Human checks | No | He sent a wrong date without checking |

    - Rina's message had 1 of the 7 fields. Each missing field led to a mistake.

    - The AI-written description was long, but it had no input instructions, no do-not-use condition, and no human checks. Long is not the same as useful.

  - **Part 3: How the three-step method fixes the handoff (20 min):**

    The instructor runs Poor Prompt 1 and Improved Prompts 1 and 2 below in front of the class and compares the answers.

    - **Step 1 fixes the missing-information problem:** Seven fixed fields make sure nothing important is left out.

    - **Step 2 fixes the "it is clear to me" problem:** The writer always understands their own card. A new reader finds the unclear parts.

    - **Step 3 fixes the unchecked-output problem:** Human checks on new input show that the card works without the writer.

    - *Gross Profit Margin:* `(Revenue - COGS) / Revenue` - the fundamental indicator of production efficiency.
    - *Operating Expenses (OPEX):* Overhead required to run the company (R&D, marketing, executive salaries, office rent).
  - **Part 2: Executive Translation - Storytelling with Financial Data (15 min):**  
    Department heads and store managers often do not have finance degrees. Business AI communication must translate changes in margin percentages into concrete operational stories (e.g., *"Our sales grew 10%, but freight costs grew 25%, causing our net profit to decline"*).
  - **Part 3: Guardrails for AI Financial Calculations (10 min):**  
    Never ask AI: *"Is our company healthy?"* (too subjective). Always provide the raw financial figures and demand explicit mathematical formulas: *"Calculate Gross Margin as (Revenue - COGS)/Revenue and display the intermediate fraction."*

- **Lecture Content (Hour 1 - 50 Minutes):**
  - **Part 1: Operational Bottlenecks & The Theory of Constraints (15 min):**  
    In manufacturing and service operations, Eliyahu Goldratt's Theory of Constraints states that any manageable system is limited in achieving more of its goals by at least one constraint (the bottleneck). Increasing speed anywhere other than the bottleneck is an illusion.
  - **Part 2: The Toyota 5-Whys Diagnostic Methodology (15 min):**  
    Developed by Sakichi Toyoda, the 5-Whys technique resolves operational problems by repeatedly asking "Why?" 5 times. This pushes past superficial symptoms (e.g., *"The driver was late"*) down to systemic management failures (e.g., *"The maintenance scheduling software lacked automated reminders"*).
  - **Part 3: Auditing AI Causal Reasoning (10 min):**  
    AI often makes "logical leaps" when doing root-cause analysis (e.g., jumping from a late delivery directly to *"the CEO lacks vision"*). Business analysts must audit each causal link: Is this a *Strong Link* (empirically probable), a *Logical Leap* (unproven jump), or *Superficial* (blaming individuals instead of systems)?

- **Reference:** [McKinsey & Company / WEF: Global Lighthouse Network in Operations](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/operations/our-insights/global-lighthouse-network) - Case studies of global facilities resolving operational bottlenecks and applying 5-Whys root cause analysis.

    ```
    *Why it fails:* ChatGPT will guess superficial reasons ("workers were tired", "the manager didn't plan ahead") without investigating underlying system constraints.
  - **Strong Prompt Example (Toyota 5-Whys Root Cause Protocol):**
    ```text
    You are a Lean Six Sigma Operations Director.
    Problem Statement: An e-commerce fulfillment warehouse experienced a 3-day shipping backlog affecting 500 customer orders during Black Friday.

    Conduct a rigorous Toyota 5-Whys diagnostic following these rules:
    - At each "Why" level, state the direct physical/operational failure, then ask the next logical question.
    - Rule: Do NOT blame individuals (e.g., "workers were careless"). Focus strictly on equipment, software, training, and standard operating procedures (SOPs).
    - Causal Audit: For each link, classify the logic as [Strong Empirical Link] or [Logical Leap to be Verified].

    ```

#### Session 4.2: Troubleshooting & Polishing Prompts

![Illustration: A planner asks, "What should the AI do when key information is missing?" while noticing the blank budget field in a campaign request.](images/session_4_2_prompt_troubleshooting_gouache.png)

* **Lecture Presentation:** [Download Slides (PDF)](slides/session_4_2_lecture.pdf)

- **Duration:** 3 Hours (180 minutes)
  - *Hour 1 (50 min):* Comprehensive Concept Lecture & Live Demonstration
  - *Hour 2 (50 min):* Guided Hands-On Lab (Basic Exercise: Defensive Fallback Bug Patch)
  - *Hour 3 (50 min):* Applied Business Challenge & Peer Debrief (Advanced Exercise: Adversarial Red-Teaming Stress Test)
- **Goal:** Test prompts with tricky inputs to see where they fail and make them more reliable.
- **Lecture Content (Hour 1 - 50 Minutes):**
  - **Part 1: Defensive Prompt Engineering (15 min):**  
    Prompts that work in a quiet classroom often fail in a busy workplace because real users enter incomplete, messy, or contradictory information. Defensive prompt engineering designs prompts to handle "edge cases" safely without crashing or hallucinating.
  - **Part 2: The "Clarification Fallback" Rule (15 min):**  
    The most critical defensive rule in enterprise prompt design:  
    `"If any required data point (like price, date, or customer name) is missing from the input, do NOT invent or assume a number. Stop immediately and ask the user 3 clarifying questions."`
  - **Part 3: Peer Quality Assurance (QA) Protocols (10 min):**  
    How software engineering QA applies to business prompts: Having a colleague test your prompt with "adversarial" tricky inputs to expose hidden failure modes before deploying the prompt to clients.

    ![Session 4.2 Conceptual Framework: Defensive Prompt Engineering and Clarification Fallbacks](images/session_4_2_key_concept.png)

  - **Part 4: Instructor Live Demonstration Script & Discussion (10 min):**  
    *Live Demo:* Take an open-source prompt from the Awesome ChatGPT Prompts GitHub repository (e.g., an advertising budget planner). Enter an edge case: *"Plan a 6-month marketing campaign for our luxury watch brand."* (Notice that no budget was provided). Show ChatGPT hallucinating a $50,000 budget. Then apply the defensive patch to force the AI to halt and ask for the budget.  
    *Discussion Question:* *"What is the business danger of an AI prompt that guesses missing information instead of asking for clarification?"* (Answer: It could cause employees to execute marketing or financial plans based on fabricated numbers, leading to major monetary waste).
- **Public Data Source:** [Awesome ChatGPT Prompts (GitHub Public Open Source)](https://github.com/f/awesome-chatgpt-prompts) - The most widely used open-source collection of community prompts for role-playing and business tasks.
- **Reference:** [OWASP Foundation: Top 10 for Large Language Model Applications](https://owasp.github.io/www-project-top-10-for-large-language-model-applications/) - Enterprise risk guide on prompt injection, hallucinations, and building clarification fallbacks when business input data is missing.
- **Prompt Comparison:**
  - **Weak Prompt Example (Vague & Unprotected against Missing Data):**
    ```text
    Plan a 6-month digital advertising campaign for our new luxury leather goods line.
    ```
    *Why it fails:* The prompt omits the total budget, target demographics, and key channels. ChatGPT hallucinates numbers (e.g., guessing a $100,000 budget), risking expensive strategic misalignment.
  - **Strong Prompt Example (Defensive Prompting with Clarification Fallback):**
    ```text
    You are a Senior Media Planner. You are evaluating a marketing campaign request.

    Client Input:
    """
    Launch a 6-month marketing campaign for our handcrafted leather handbags. Target high-income professionals.
    """

    DEFENSIVE QUALITY GATE (MANDATORY RULE):
    Before drafting any campaign strategy, check whether the input contains the following 3 mandatory data points:
    1. Total allocated advertising budget.
    2. Target customer age bracket and primary geographic market.
    3. Specific performance target (e.g., Target ROAS, sales volume, or email signups).

    IF ANY of these 3 data points are missing:
    - Do NOT fabricate a budget or assume target numbers.
    - Halt immediately and ask the user the 3 specific clarifying questions needed to complete the brief.
    ```
    *Why it succeeds:* Builds a "Clarification Fallback" circuit breaker that halts execution when critical business inputs are missing, eliminating hallucinated budgets.
- **How to Practice:**
  1. **Step 1:** Open the Awesome ChatGPT Prompts repository and choose one business role prompt (e.g., "Act as an Advertiser" or "Act as an Accountant").
  2. **Step 2:** Test the chosen prompt in ChatGPT with complete, normal business information.
  3. **Step 3:** Test the exact same prompt with a tricky edge-case input (e.g., leave out the budget number, give contradictory deadlines, or provide missing customer data).
  4. **Step 4:** Observe whether ChatGPT hallucinated fake numbers or gave unsafe advice. Write a defensive prompt patch (e.g., adding: *"If any required information is missing, do not guess; ask 3 clarifying questions first"*). Log this in your Prompt Bug Report.
- **What to Expect:** Discover that AI will often guess or invent answers if data is missing, and learn how to instruct it to ask questions instead.

Observe hallucinated values, diagnose the failure mode, and write a defensive "Clarification Fallback" prompt patch instructing the AI to halt and ask clarifying questions.
  - Deliverable: Completed Prompt Bug Report documenting the baseline prompt, the edge-case test applied, observed AI hallucinations, and the revised defensive prompt code.

  - Scenario: Using the exact same business prompt role and scenario from Exercise 1, conduct a rigorous adversarial red-teaming audit by subjecting the patched prompt to three severe enterprise stress cases: (1) Contradictory constraints (e.g., "fast turnaround in 2 hours with zero budget"), (2) Malicious prompt injection attempting to override system guidelines, and (3) Incomplete / corrupted input data.
